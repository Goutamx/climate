📌 Basic Concepts

Weather vs Climate

  • Weather → Momentary state of the atmosphere (changes within a day or a week).
  • Climate → Average of weather conditions over a longer period of time (changes usually noted after 50 years or more).
  • Elements of weather → Temperature, pressure, wind direction and velocity, humidity and precipitation.

Monsoon

  • The word “Monsoon” refers to a climate associated with seasonal reversal in the direction of winds.
  • India has a hot monsoonal climate, typical of South and Southeast Asia.

📌 Unity and Diversity in the Monsoon Climate

Unity

  • The monsoon regime emphasises the unity of India with the rest of the Southeast Asian region.
  • Despite regional variations, India’s climate is monsoonal in rhythm and character.

Diversity (Regional Variations)

Regional diversities are expressed as sub‑types of monsoon climate in terms of:

  • Pattern of winds
  • Temperature and rainfall
  • Rhythm of seasons
  • Degree of wetness or dryness

📌 Factors Determining the Climate of India

1. Latitude

  • The Tropic of Cancer passes through central India in an east–west direction.
  • North of the Tropic of Cancer → Sub‑tropical / temperate zone → extreme climate with high daily and annual range of temperature.
  • South of the Tropic of Cancer → Tropical zone, nearer the equator → high temperatures throughout the year with small daily and annual range.

2. The Himalayan Mountains

  • Act as an effective climatic divide.
  • Provide an invincible shield against cold northern winds originating near the Arctic Circle and blowing across Central and Eastern Asia.
  • Trap the monsoon winds and force them to shed most of their moisture within the Indian subcontinent.

3. Distribution of Land and Water

  • India is flanked by the Indian Ocean on three sides and a continuous mountain wall to the north.
  • Water bodies heat up and cool down more slowly than land.
  • This causes differential heating of land and sea, creating different pressure zones in different seasons and leading to reversal of monsoon winds.

4. Distance from the Sea

  • India’s long coastline ensures that coastal areas have an equable climate.
  • Interior locations far from the sea experience extremes of climate.
  • Examples: Mumbai and the Konkan coast hardly feel extremes; Delhi, Kanpur and Amritsar face sharp seasonal contrasts.

5. Altitude

  • Temperature decreases with height.
  • Thin air at high altitudes → cooler mountains than plains.
  • Example: Agra and Darjiling lie on nearly the same latitude, but in January Agra: 16°C; Darjiling: 4°C.

6. Relief

Relief affects temperature, air pressure, direction and speed of wind, and amount & distribution of rainfall.

  • Windward sides of Western Ghats and Assam receive heavy rainfall (June–September).
  • The southern plateau remains comparatively dry due to its leeward position with respect to the Western Ghats.

📌 The Nature of Indian Monsoon

The monsoon is a familiar but still not fully understood climatic phenomenon. No single theory explains it completely. A real breakthrough came when the monsoon was studied on a global rather than purely regional scale.

Two important aspects:

  • The onset of the monsoon
  • The break in the monsoon

📌 Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)

Feature Details
Definition A low‑pressure zone located near the equator where trade winds converge and air tends to ascend.
July position Around 20°–25°N (over the Gangetic plain), called the monsoon trough.
Function Encourages development of a thermal low over north and northwest India.
Mechanism With the northward shift of ITCZ, southeast trade winds of the southern hemisphere cross the equator between 40° and 60°E, get deflected due to Coriolis force, and blow as the southwest monsoon.
Winter ITCZ shifts southward → winds reverse from northeast towards south and southwest → northeast monsoons.

📌 Onset of the Monsoon & El‑Niño

Onset of the Monsoon – 19th Century Mechanism

  1. Differential heating of land and sea during summer sets the stage.
  2. In April–May, the sun shines vertically over the Tropic of Cancer.
  3. The large landmass to the north of the Indian Ocean becomes intensely heated.
  4. A cell of intense low pressure develops over the northwestern part of the subcontinent.
  5. Over the relatively cooler Indian Ocean, high pressure persists.
  6. The low‑pressure cell attracts southeast trades across the equator.
  7. These conditions favour the northward shift of ITCZ.
  8. The southwest monsoon is thus seen as a continuation of the southeast trades deflected towards the Indian subcontinent after crossing the equator.
  9. These winds cross the equator between 40°E and 60°E longitudes.

Role of Jet Streams

  • The shift of ITCZ is associated with the withdrawal of the westerly jet stream from its position south of the Himalayas over the north Indian plain.
  • An easterly jet stream sets in along about 15°N latitude only after the westerly jet withdraws.
  • The easterly jet stream is mainly responsible for the burst of monsoon over India.

Entry of Monsoon into India

  • SW monsoon sets in over the Kerala coast by 1st June.
  • It advances rapidly to reach Mumbai and Kolkata between 10th and 13th June.
  • By mid‑July, it covers the entire subcontinent.

📌 El‑Niño and the Indian Monsoon

Feature Details
Definition Complex weather system appearing once every 3–7 years.
Effects Brings droughts, floods and weather extremes to many parts of the world.
Mechanism Warm currents appear off the coast of Peru in the eastern Pacific, an extension of warm equatorial current temporarily replacing the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current.
Temperature effect Increases water temperature off Peru by about 10°C.
Results (i) Distortion of equatorial atmospheric circulation (ii) Irregularities in evaporation of seawater (iii) Reduction in plankton → decline in fish.

Example 1990–91 wild El‑Niño event → onset of SW monsoon delayed 5–12 days over most parts of India.

📌 Break in the Monsoon

During the SW monsoon, if after a few days of rain the rain fails for one or more weeks, it is called a break in the monsoon. Such dry spells are quite common.

Causes in Different Regions

  • Northern India → Breaks occur when rain‑bearing storms are not frequent along the monsoon trough/ITCZ.
  • West Coast → Dry spells occur when winds blow parallel to the coast, preventing uplift.

📌 Understanding the Monsoon (Southern Oscillation)

The intensity of SW monsoon winds is related to the Southern Oscillation, measured by the difference in air pressure between:

  • Tahiti (about 20°S, 140°W) in French Polynesia, eastern Pacific, and
  • Port Darwin (12°30′S, 131°E) in northern Australia.

The Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) uses 16 indicators to forecast monsoon behaviour.

📌 Rhythm of Seasons

Indian meteorologists recognise four seasons:

  1. The Cold Weather Season
  2. The Hot Weather Season
  3. The Southwest Monsoon Season
  4. The Retreating Monsoon Season

📌 Season 1: The Cold Weather Season

Temperature

  • Sets in by mid‑November in northern India.
  • December and January are the coldest months in the northern plains.
  • Mean daily temperature is generally below 21°C over most of northern India.
  • Night temperature may fall below freezing in Punjab and Rajasthan.

Three Reasons for Excessive Cold in North India

  1. Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan are far from the moderating influence of the sea → continental climate.
  2. Snowfall in the nearby Himalayas intensifies cold waves.
  3. Around February, cold winds from the Caspian Sea and Turkmenistan bring cold wave, frost and fog over northwestern India.

Peninsular India

  • Experiences no well‑defined cold season.
  • Little seasonal change in coastal temperature due to maritime influence and proximity to the equator.
  • Example: Thiruvananthapuram – January mean max: 21°C; June: 29.5°C.
  • Hills of the Western Ghats have comparatively lower temperatures.

Pressure and Winds

  • By late December (~22nd December), the sun is overhead the Tropic of Capricorn.
  • Weather over northern plains: feeble high pressure.
  • Air pressure is slightly lower over south India.
  • Isobars: about 1019 mb over NW India and 1013 mb over far south.
  • Winds blow from the northwestern high towards the low pressure over the Indian Ocean.
  • Due to weak pressure gradient, winds are light (velocity 3–5 km/hour).
  • Direction:
    • Westerly or north‑westerly down the Ganga valley,
    • Northerly in the Ganga–Brahmaputra delta,
    • North‑easterly over Bay of Bengal, uninfluenced by topography.

Winter Weather Disturbances

  • Pleasant winter weather is periodically disturbed by shallow cyclonic depressions.
  • Origin: East Mediterranean Sea.
  • Path: move eastwards across West Asia, Iran, Afghanistan and Pakistan to reach NW India.
  • Gain moisture from Caspian Sea and Persian Gulf; steered by westerly jet streams.

Rainfall in the Cold Weather Season

Winter monsoon winds generally do not cause rainfall as they move from land to sea, carry little humidity and are associated with anti‑cyclonic circulation.

Important Exceptions

Region Details
NW India Weak temperate cyclones from Mediterranean bring rainfall to Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, western UP; meagre but vital for rabi crops. Also snowfall in lower Himalayas sustaining rivers; precipitation decreases west → east in plains and north → south in mountains. Delhi average winter rainfall ≈ 53 mm.
Punjab & Bihar Receive about 25 mm and 18 mm rain respectively.
Central India Central India and northern parts of southern peninsula get occasional winter rainfall.
NE India Arunachal Pradesh & Assam → 25–50 mm rain during winter.
Tamil Nadu coast In October–November, the NE monsoon crosses the Bay of Bengal, gathers moisture and brings torrential rainfall over Tamil Nadu coast, southern Andhra Pradesh, SE Karnataka and SE Kerala.

📌 Season 2: The Hot Weather Season

Temperature

  • From March, the sun shifts towards the Tropic of Cancer and temperatures rise over north India.
  • April, May and June are peak summer months in north India with most places between 30–32°C.
Month Temperature Details
March Highest day temperature around 38°C in the Deccan Plateau.
April Temperatures of 38–43°C in Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh.
May Heat belt shifts north; in NW India, readings of 48°C are not uncommon.

South India

  • Hot weather is mild compared to north India.
  • Peninsular position and oceanic influence keep temperatures lower.
  • Temperatures generally between 26–32°C; Western Ghats hills often below 25°C.
  • Coastal isotherms run parallel to the coast; from coast to interior, temperature increases instead of decreasing north–south.
  • Mean daily minima rarely fall below 26°C.

Pressure and Winds

  • Excessive heating and falling pressure in the northern half of India.
  • ITCZ shifts northwards; by July, its axis is near 25°N, forming an elongated monsoon trough from the Thar Desert to Patna and Chotanagpur.
  • This trough attracts surface winds:
    • South‑westerlies along the west coast and Bengal/Bangladesh coast.
    • Easterly or south‑easterly winds over north Bengal and Bihar.
  • These winds are actually “displaced” equatorial easterlies. Their strong inflow by mid‑June heralds the rainy season.

Loo

  • Loo are dry, hot winds blowing in the heart of the monsoon trough over northwestern plains.
  • Blow in the afternoon, often continuing till midnight.
  • Hot, dry and oppressive; prevalent from Punjab to Bihar, strongest between Delhi and Patna.

Dust Storms

  • Very common in May in Punjab, Haryana, eastern Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh.
  • Often bring temporary relief from heat, with light rain and cool breeze.

Local Storms

Moisture‑laden winds attracted towards the periphery of the trough meet dry air masses → sudden contact produces local storms of great intensity, with violent winds, torrential rain and hail.

Some Famous Local Storms of the Hot Weather Season

Storm Details
Mango Shower Pre‑monsoon showers towards end of summer in Kerala and coastal Karnataka; help in early ripening of mangoes.
Blossom Shower Showers which make coffee flowers blossom in Kerala and nearby areas.
Nor Westers Dreaded evening thunderstorms in Bengal and Assam; local name “Kalbaisakhi” (calamity of month Baisakh); useful for tea, jute and rice cultivation. In Assam called “Bardoisila”.
Loo Hot, dry and oppressive winds over northern plains from Punjab to Bihar, strongest between Delhi and Patna.

📌 Season 3: The Southwest Monsoon Season

Onset Mechanism

  1. Rapid temperature rise in May over NW plains intensifies low pressure.
  2. By early June, pressure is low enough to attract SE trade winds from the southern hemisphere.
  3. These winds cross the equator, enter the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea.
  4. Passing over equatorial warm currents, they pick up abundant moisture.
  5. After crossing the equator they are deflected and blow in a south‑westerly direction → called the southwest monsoon.

Burst of Monsoon

  • Rain begins rather abruptly, with violent thunder and lightning.
  • First rains sharply lower temperature by 5–8°C between mid‑June and mid‑July.
  • Sudden onset is popularly called the “burst” or “break” of the monsoon.
  • Coastal Kerala, Karnataka, Goa and Maharashtra often get the burst in the first week of June; interiors may receive it only by first week of July.

Two Main Branches

  • The Arabian Sea branch
  • The Bay of Bengal branch

Monsoon Winds of the Arabian Sea

The Arabian Sea branch splits into three sub‑branches.

Branch 1: Western Ghats Branch

  • Strikes the Western Ghats and is forced to ascend 900–1200 m.
  • Cooling causes very heavy rainfall (250–400 cm) on the windward side – Sahyadris and Western Coastal Plain.
  • Descending on the leeward side, winds warm up and lose humidity → rain‑shadow conditions east of the Ghats (e.g. Pune, Bengaluru receive far less rain than Kozhikode, Mangalore).

Branch 2: Narmada–Tapi Valley Branch

  • Strikes the coast north of Mumbai.
  • Moves along Narmada and Tapi valleys, giving rainfall to large parts of central India.
  • Chotanagpur plateau receives about 15 cm from this branch.
  • Then enters the Ganga plains and merges with the Bay of Bengal branch.

Branch 3: Saurashtra–Kachchh Branch

  • Strikes Saurashtra Peninsula and Kachchh, then travels over west Rajasthan along the Aravalis.
  • Produces only scanty rainfall.
  • In Punjab and Haryana, this branch joins the Bay of Bengal branch; their combined flow gives rain to western Himalayas.

Monsoon Winds of the Bay of Bengal

  • Initially strikes the coast of Myanmar and parts of SE Bangladesh.
  • Arakan Hills deflect much of the current towards the Indian subcontinent.
  • Monsoon enters West Bengal and Bangladesh from the south and southeast, not directly from SW.

Two Sub‑branches

  • One moves westward along the Ganga plains, as far as the Punjab plains.
  • Another moves up the Brahmaputra valley towards the north and northeast, giving widespread rains; a sub‑branch strikes Garo and Khasi hills where Mawsynram receives the highest average annual rainfall in the world.

Why Tamil Nadu Coast Remains Dry in SW Monsoon

  • Coast lies parallel to the Bay of Bengal branch → winds do not strike it directly.
  • Lies in the rain‑shadow of the Arabian Sea branch behind the Western Ghats.

📌 Season 4: Season of Retreating Monsoon

Timeline of Retreat

Time Area
End of September SW monsoon weakens; low‑pressure trough shifts southward.
First week of September Withdrawal from western Rajasthan.
End of September Retreat completed from Rajasthan, Gujarat, western Ganga plain, Central Highlands.
Beginning of October Low pressure remains over northern Bay of Bengal.
Early November Moves over Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.
Mid‑December Centre of low pressure removed from entire peninsula – retreat completed.

Characteristics

  • Clear skies and a rise in temperature.
  • Land is still moist; high temperature + humidity cause “October heat”.
  • In second half of October, mercury falls rapidly in northern India.

Rainfall Pattern

  • North India → generally dry.
  • Eastern Peninsula (especially Tamil Nadu and adjoining areas) → experiences rain.
  • October–November are the rainiest months for the eastern part of the peninsula.

Cyclonic Activity

  • Heavy rainfall associated with cyclonic depressions.
  • These originate in the Andaman Sea and cross the eastern coast of southern peninsula as tropical cyclones.
  • Thickly populated deltas of Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri are frequent targets; some also strike West Bengal, Bangladesh and Myanmar.
  • Coromandal coast receives much of its rainfall from these systems.
  • Cyclones in the Arabian Sea are less frequent.

📌 Distribution of Rainfall in India

Average annual rainfall in India is about 125 cm, but with great spatial variation.

Category Rainfall Areas
High Rainfall Above 200 cm West coast, Western Ghats, sub‑Himalayan NE areas, hills of Meghalaya. Khasi & Jaintia hills exceed 1,000 cm; Brahmaputra valley receives slightly less than 200 cm.
Medium Rainfall 100–200 cm Southern Gujarat, east Tamil Nadu, NE peninsula (Odisha, Jharkhand, Bihar, eastern MP), northern Ganga plain along sub‑Himalayas, Cachar Valley, Manipur.
Low Rainfall 50–100 cm Western UP, Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, J&K, eastern Rajasthan, Gujarat, much of Deccan Plateau.
Inadequate Rainfall Below 50 cm Parts of peninsula (Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra), Ladakh and most of western Rajasthan.

Snowfall in India is largely confined to the Himalayan region.

📌 Monsoons and Economic Life in India

  1. Monsoon is the axis around which India’s agricultural cycle revolves. About 64% of the population depends on agriculture based mainly on SW monsoon.
  2. Except the Himalayas, temperatures in most parts remain above the threshold for crop growth throughout the year.
  3. Regional variations in monsoon climate permit cultivation of various types of crops.
  4. Variability of rainfall often results in droughts or floods in different parts each year.
  5. Agricultural prosperity depends on timely and well‑distributed rainfall; monsoon failure seriously affects agriculture, especially where irrigation is limited.
  6. Sudden monsoon bursts can cause serious soil erosion over large areas.
  7. Winter rainfall from temperate cyclones is highly beneficial for rabi crops in north India.
  8. Climatic diversity is reflected in the diversity of food habits, clothing and house types across the country.

📌 Global Warming

Background

  • Climate has changed in the past at both global and local scales, though changes are often imperceptible during a human lifetime.
  • Geological evidence indicates that large parts of the earth were once under ice cover.

Causes

  • Natural causes.
  • Human activities such as large‑scale industrialisation and emission of polluting gases.

Greenhouse Gases

Gas Details
Carbon dioxide (CO₂) Produced mainly by burning fossil fuels; increasing steadily and is a major concern.
Methane Present in smaller concentrations.
Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) Present in smaller concentrations.
Nitrous oxide Present in smaller concentrations.

These gases are better absorbers of long‑wave radiation than CO₂ and are therefore more effective in enhancing the greenhouse effect, contributing to global warming. Continued warming could melt polar ice caps and mountain glaciers, raising sea levels.

Consequences

Mean annual surface temperature has already increased in the last 150 years. By 2100, global temperature is projected to rise by about 2°C.

Consequence Details
Sea level rise Average rise of about 48 cm by the end of the 21st century due to melting of glaciers and sea‑ice.
Flooding Increase in incidence of annual flooding.
Diseases Favourable conditions for insect‑borne diseases such as malaria.
Climatic boundaries Shift in climatic zones; some regions become wetter, others drier.
Agriculture Shifts in agricultural patterns and crop suitability.
Ecosystems Human populations and ecosystems would face major changes and stresses.

📌 Exam Cheat Sheet – Key Questions & Answers

A compact set of exam‑style questions with ready answers based on the complete notes above. Use this page for final revision.

Q1. Differentiate between weather and climate.
  • Weather is the momentary state of the atmosphere at a place – it can change within a day or a week. It is described by elements like temperature, pressure, wind, humidity and precipitation.
  • Climate is the average of weather conditions over a long period of time (normally 30–50 years or more). Changes in climate are noticed only after several decades.
Q2. Explain the unity and diversity of the monsoon climate of India.

The monsoon regime emphasises the unity of India with the rest of South and Southeast Asia: most of the country experiences rainfall from the seasonal reversal of winds known as the monsoon; the rhythm of advancing and retreating monsoon is broadly similar everywhere.

At the same time, there is great diversity which appears as different sub‑types of monsoon climate. These are expressed in:

  • Patterns of winds
  • Temperature and rainfall amounts
  • Rhythm and duration of seasons
  • Degree of wetness or dryness

Extreme examples include temperatures from 55°C in western Rajasthan to −45°C around Leh, and rainfall from over 1,080 cm at Mawsynram to about 9 cm at Jaisalmer.

Q3. List the major factors determining the climate of India.
  1. Latitude – division by the Tropic of Cancer into tropical south and sub‑tropical/temperate north.
  2. The Himalayan Mountains – act as a climatic divide, blocking cold northern winds and trapping monsoon winds.
  3. Distribution of land and water – contrast between vast landmass and surrounding Indian Ocean causes differential heating, pressure belts and reversal of winds.
  4. Distance from the sea – coastal areas have equable climate; interiors show extremes.
  5. Altitude – temperature decreases with height; hence mountains are cooler than plains.
  6. Relief – controls temperature, pressure, winds and rainfall (e.g. windward vs leeward sides of Western Ghats).
Q4. What is the Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)? How is it related to the Indian monsoon?

The ITCZ is a low‑pressure zone located near the equator where the trade winds from both hemispheres converge and air tends to ascend.

  • In July, it shifts to about 20°–25°N over the Gangetic plain, forming the monsoon trough.
  • Its northward shift encourages a thermal low over north and northwest India and allows the southeast trades of the southern hemisphere to cross the equator, get deflected, and blow as the southwest monsoon.
  • In winter the ITCZ moves southward and winds reverse to become northeast monsoons.
Q5. What is El‑Niño? How does it affect the Indian monsoon?

El‑Niño is a complex weather system appearing once every 3–7 years when a tongue of warm water spreads along the coast of Peru in the eastern Pacific, temporarily replacing the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current.

  • Raises sea‑surface temperature by about 10°C off Peru.
  • Distorts equatorial atmospheric circulation, affects evaporation and reduces plankton.
  • Causes droughts, floods and weather extremes in many parts of the world, including irregularities in the Indian southwest monsoon.
  • Example: during 1990–91 El‑Niño, onset of the SW monsoon over many parts of India was delayed by 5–12 days.
Q6. What is meant by “break in the monsoon”?

During the southwest monsoon season, if after several days of rain there is a period of one or more weeks with little or no rain, it is called a break in the monsoon. In northern India it occurs when rain‑bearing storms are absent along the monsoon trough/ITCZ, and on the west coast when winds blow parallel to the coast.

Q7. Name and briefly describe the four seasons recognised by Indian meteorologists.
  1. Cold Weather Season – mid‑November to February; dominated by cool, dry conditions, weak high pressure over northern plains and occasional western disturbances.
  2. Hot Weather Season – March to May/early June; increasing temperatures, formation of low pressure over northwest India, local storms (Nor‑westers, Mango showers, Loo, etc.).
  3. Southwest Monsoon Season – June to September; onset and widespread rainfall from the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal branches of SW monsoon.
  4. Retreating Monsoon Season – October to December; withdrawal of SW monsoon, clear skies, “October heat”, and cyclonic rainfall over eastern and southeastern coasts.

Q9. Why is the Tamil Nadu coast generally dry during the southwest monsoon?
  • The Tamil Nadu coast lies parallel to the Bay of Bengal branch of the southwest monsoon, so winds do not strike it directly.
  • It also lies in the rain‑shadow area of the Western Ghats with respect to the Arabian Sea branch.
Q10. Explain briefly how monsoons affect the economic life of India.
  • The southwest monsoon is the axis of the agricultural cycle; about 64% of India’s people depend directly or indirectly on agriculture.
  • Temperature is high enough almost everywhere to grow crops throughout the year; it is mainly the amount, distribution and reliability of rainfall that control success or failure of crops.
  • Regional variations in monsoon climate permit cultivation of a wide variety of crops.
  • Variability leads to droughts and floods, affecting food security and economy.
  • Sudden heavy monsoon bursts can cause soil erosion.
  • Winter rainfall from western disturbances is vital for rabi crops in north India.
  • Climatic diversity is reflected in the diversity of food, clothing and house types across India.
Q11. What is global warming? Mention some of its likely consequences.

Global warming is the long‑term increase in the earth’s mean surface temperature due to enhanced greenhouse effect, largely caused by human‑produced greenhouse gases such as CO₂, methane, CFCs and nitrous oxide.

Consequence Details
Sea level rise Average rise of about 48 cm by the end of 21st century.
Flooding Increase in frequency and extent of annual floods.
Diseases Expansion of insect‑borne diseases such as malaria into new areas.
Climatic boundaries Shift of climatic zones; some regions become wetter, others drier.
Agriculture Shifts in cropping patterns and agricultural productivity.
Ecosystems & people Stress on ecosystems and human populations due to temperature rise and extreme events.
